Over the years, I have developed a strong affinity for solving problems in production systems through automation, making mechatronics a natural choice for my undergraduate studies. My experience in the discipline brought mechanics, electronics, control systems, and software into a single field of view. What continues to interest me is how these elements interact—and how the same systems thinking can be applied beyond physical machines.

That curiosity eventually expanded toward computer systems and their application to business problems. My work is most engaging when technology moves beyond novelty and becomes useful: improving a process, removing friction, supporting better decisions, or enabling work that would otherwise be difficult to scale. The tools may change, but the underlying challenge remains consistent: understand the system, identify where value is being lost, and find a practical way to improve the outcome.

## Learning from first principles

Curiosity has always drawn me beyond the immediate task toward the principles behind it. Books provide one path into those principles, offering knowledge, perspectives, and questions that can be carried into practical work.

My preferred approach to learning begins with first principles: examine the assumptions, understand the fundamentals, and build upward from what can be explained clearly. Rather than accepting an idea only because it is familiar or widely repeated, the aim is to understand why it works, where it applies, and where its limits begin.

Philosophy helps me ask better questions and examine the assumptions beneath an argument. Mathematics brings structure and precision to reasoning. The scientific method provides a disciplined way to test ideas against evidence. Together, these foundations support a broader purpose: transforming knowledge into understanding, and understanding into something valuable.

## Entrepreneurship and leadership

Entrepreneurship brings many of these interests together. My ambition is to create ventures around worthwhile problems, particularly where technology, automation, and business strategy intersect. What attracts me is the complete journey: recognizing a genuine need, shaping an effective solution, and building an organization capable of delivering that solution well.

Through that process, I hope to develop not only as an entrepreneur but also as a leader. Leadership, in my view, is a practice rather than a title. Sound judgment, clear communication, responsibility for outcomes, and the ability to help others succeed matter more than status.

At its best, leadership creates the conditions in which people can contribute meaningfully and move toward a shared objective. This blog forms part of that development by giving my ideas somewhere to become explicit, encounter criticism, and improve over time.

## Beyond work

Chess appeals to me because it rewards patience, strategic thinking, and careful decisions under uncertainty. The game offers a compact expression of a broader lesson: every decision changes the position, and good judgment depends on understanding both the immediate move and its longer-term consequences.

Football, basketball, and tennis provide different expressions of competition, discipline, teamwork, and individual performance. My enjoyment comes not only from the contests themselves, but also from observing how preparation, tactics, and execution shape the result.

Literature provides another space for reflection. Rudyard Kipling remains my favorite author, with *If—* offering an enduring meditation on character, composure, and perseverance. My favorite book is John Bunyan’s *The Pilgrim’s Progress*, whose portrayal of conviction and the difficult journey toward a worthy destination continues to resonate with me.

The work of Ralph Waldo Emerson and Napoleon Hill also contributes ideas about self-reliance, purpose, initiative, and the relationship between thought and action. Across these different influences, a common theme remains: character is developed through disciplined action, especially when circumstances make that discipline difficult.
